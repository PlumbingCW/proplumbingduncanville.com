Leaking Slab Repair in Duncanville, TX

How to recognize a leaking slab and what the repair options mean.

Call (972) 903-8224

A leaking slab is a concrete foundation with a water pipe leaking in or under it. The slab itself does not leak. The pipe does, and the water finds its way up through cracks or soaks the soil. The result is a warm floor, a higher bill or a damp spot that appears for no reason.

This guide covers how to recognize the signs, how plumbers confirm a slab leak without tearing up the floor and which repair options exist. It is aimed at Duncanville homeowners who suspect a problem and want to know what to expect before they call.

Signs your slab may be leaking

  • A hot spot on the floor, which suggests a hot water line
  • A meter that moves when every fixture is off
  • A bill that rose with no change in habits
  • The sound of running water at night
  • Damp carpet, lifting laminate or cracked tile with no source
  • Water pooling at the foundation edge
  • Low hot water pressure with normal cold pressure

No single sign proves a slab leak. A running toilet, a leaking irrigation line or a failed water heater can cause some of the same signs. That is why confirmation matters before any concrete is cut.

Why slabs and pipes fail together in this region

According to the USDA Official Series Description for Houston Black clay, a Blackland Prairie soil, when dry the soil develops cracks 0.5 to 4 inches wide to a depth of 30 centimeters or more, and the cracks stay open for 90 to 150 cumulative days in most years.

The soil is 40 to 60 percent clay. That is a profile of clay soils common in the Dallas region, not a map of any one Duncanville lot.

Clay of this type swells in wet months and shrinks in dry ones. A slab and the pipes under it move with it, even if the movement is small. A rigid pipe can rub on concrete or at a bend until it wears through. Copper can also corrode where it touches certain soils and concrete.

How a plumber confirms the leak

  1. 01Check the meterWith every fixture off, a moving meter dial confirms water is leaving the system.
  2. 02Isolate the lineClosing valves one by one shows whether the leak is on the hot line, the cold line or the service line.
  3. 03Listen and scanAcoustic equipment hears the leak, and thermal imaging shows warm patches.
  4. 04Mark the spotA mark on the floor shows the target so a small opening is made, not a trench.

A reroute is often the quietest option. New lines run overhead and away from the slab. They are also easier to reach if a leak comes back. A spot repair is cheaper for a single leak, but the next one under the same slab is likely to follow if the pipe is old.

Questions to ask before you call anyone

  1. Has the meter been tested with every fixture off, and what did it show?
  2. Is the leak on the hot side, the cold side or the line from the meter?
  3. How will the leak be pinpointed before any concrete is cut?
  4. What repair options will you offer, and in what order?
  5. Who repairs the floor and the slab, and is that in the price?

A company that cannot answer these questions has not seen the problem yet. A good plumber will welcome them, because clear questions lead to a clear scope.

Finally, keep a record. A simple log of your meter readings, the date you first noticed the signs and any repairs or calls to the utility helps your plumber and your insurer.

It also shows whether the repair worked, because a meter that holds still after the work is the clearest proof that the leak is gone. Most people do not think of this until the claim is already open, so start the log the day you suspect a problem.

What to do while you wait

If you suspect a slab leak, turn off the water at the main when you leave the house, and turn it back on when you return. That limits damage if the leak is serious. Keep photographs of any damage. Take a reading of your meter morning and night, because a record helps a plumber and an insurer.

Do not hammer or drill the slab to find the pipe. You can hit a line and make the leak worse. A locator finds pipe without damage. If you need quick help, our leak detection page explains how we find the leak first.

After the repair

Ask for a pressure test and keep the report. If the floor was opened, plan for the finish: tile, laminate or carpet. Ask whether the plumber or another contractor handles that. A repaired slab should be dry. If moisture remains, a drying service may be needed.
