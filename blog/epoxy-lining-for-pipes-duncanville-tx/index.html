Epoxy Lining for Pipes in Duncanville, TX

How epoxy lining works, what it can and cannot fix and what to ask.

Call (972) 903-8224

Epoxy lining coats the inside of an existing pipe with a resin that hardens into a new inner surface. It is sold as a way to seal pinholes, stop corrosion and avoid replacing pipe. The idea is sound for some pipes in some conditions. It is not a cure for every failing pipe, and a lining is only as good as the pipe it goes into.

This guide explains how lining works, which pipes it suits, what to ask and what the limits are. It is written for Duncanville homeowners who are weighing lining against a repipe or a dig.

How epoxy lining works

The plumber first cleans the pipe, usually with air, abrasive media or water, to remove scale and deposits. Then a liquid epoxy is pushed or pulled through the pipe and cured. The cured epoxy forms a smooth, continuous layer along the wall.

The process is used for supply lines and also for drain and sewer pipes, although the materials and methods differ. For a sewer line, the common form is a resin soaked liner pulled into the pipe and cured in place. For supply lines, a thinner coating is applied to the interior.

Where lining works well

  • A pipe that is still round and structurally sound but has rough, scaled walls
  • A pipe with small cracks or open joints and no collapse
  • A line under a driveway, patio or mature landscape that you want to protect
  • A pipe with several fittings that can be cleared and sealed

Lining shines when the pipe is in decent shape and the surface is the problem. It does not replace a pipe that has lost wall thickness. A pipe that is mostly rust has little left to bond to.

Where lining does not work

Lining does not fix a dropped joint, a collapsed section or a belly, because the lining follows the shape of the pipe. It also does not help when the pipe is leaking at the fittings and the fittings cannot be sealed. Some supply lines have too many tees and bends to coat evenly.

The result also depends on prep. If the pipe is not cleaned fully, the epoxy will not bond. A contractor who rushes the cleaning or skips a camera inspection is gambling with your money.

Questions to ask before you agree

  1. What does the camera show about my pipe, and can I see the video?
  2. Is the product certified for drinking water if it is a supply line? NSF/ANSI 61 is the standard for drinking water system components, so ask for the listing.
  3. How is the pipe cleaned and tested before and after?
  4. What is the warranty, and who stands behind the product?
  5. What happens at fittings, valves and fixtures?
  6. What is the written price, and what is excluded?

Drinking water contact is the most important question. Any product used inside a supply line should be certified for potable water. Ask for the paperwork.

Cost and disruption compared with the alternatives

Lining is often chosen because it limits digging and demolition. It can leave a lawn, a driveway or a bathroom wall untouched. That has value, but it is not always cheaper than a repair, and it is not always the right answer.

A pipe that is sound enough to line may also be sound enough to clean and monitor. A pipe that is too far gone will not hold a liner.

Ask the contractor to price the options side by side: clean and monitor, line and replace. When you see the three numbers together, the decision usually becomes clear.

Red flags in a lining sales pitch

  • A promise that lining lasts forever or never fails
  • A quote given without a camera inspection
  • No certification for drinking water on a supply line product
  • Pressure to sign the same day
  • A refusal to put the warranty in writing

One more point is worth saying plainly. Lining is a tool, and tools have a purpose. Contractors who sell lining often do it well, and some sell it for jobs where a simple repair would last.

Ask for the camera video, ask what would happen without the work and ask what the alternative costs. If the answers are clear and consistent, you can trust the plan. If they are vague, get a second opinion before you sign.

Duncanville service lines and lining

The City of Duncanville completed a water service line survey on November 13, 2024, with extra attention on homes built before 1988, and describes its lines as primarily non lead or unknown. The service line from the meter to your house is a separate pipe from the plumbing inside, so ask Public Works at (972) 780-4900 before assuming what yours is made of.

Permit rules vary, so ask Permit and Inspection Services at (972) 780-5000 whether lining needs a permit. To verify a plumber, use tsbpe.texas.gov.

Lining versus replacing

A repipe replaces the pipe and gives you a known new material. Lining keeps the pipe and gives it a new inner surface. Each has a place. A house with a failing but accessible supply system may be better served by a repipe. A sewer lateral under a finished yard may be better served by lining.

We start every conversation with a camera. If lining fits, we say so. If it does not, we explain why. Our sewer line repair page covers the methods we offer. Homeowners in Lancaster and Grand Prairie with older homes often ask about lining.

Care after lining

A lined pipe still needs good habits. Keep grease and wipes out of the drain. Have the line inspected again after a few years. Keep the paperwork and the final video. If you sell the house, they show that the work was done properly.
